Picture yourself 40,000 years in the past. The mammoths didn't get you today. The cold didn't either. Now the sun slips behind the hills, the fire crackles, and the night stretches out ahead of you. Six long hours with no phone, no feed, no screen of any kind. Nothing to do at all. And strangely, that nothing is exactly what's going to make you invent fun. Music, gambling, board games, even the first movie night. All of it starts right here, with a bored human sitting in the dark.

Here's the thing most of us get wrong about our ancestors. We tend to imagine life back then as one endless emergency. Saber-toothed cats behind every rock, frostbite, hunger, a fight for survival from sunrise to sunset. Hollywood loves that version. The evidence doesn't.

When anthropologists sat down with modern hunter-gatherer groups and actually tallied up the hours, the results were surprising. Hunting, foraging, hauling water, sharpening tools. All the essential work added up to somewhere around four to six hours a day. After that, the job was basically finished. The afternoon was free. That's a shorter workday than most of us have today.

Which leaves you with a strange problem. Your brain is an expensive organ. It eats up roughly a fifth of your body's energy, and it hates sitting idle. It's built to search for patterns and crave something new. Give it hours of daylight and a long, dark evening with nothing in them, and it starts to protest. Staring at the ground isn't going to cut it.

That restless feeling has a name. Boredom. And far from being a modern problem, boredom may be one of the oldest forces pushing our species forward.

Its first big invention? Music.

In a cave in southern Germany's Swabian Jura, researchers uncovered something remarkable: a flute carved from mammoth tusk, around 40,000 years old. Mammoth ivory is tough, dense material, a nightmare to work even with today's metal tools. Yet someone armed only with sharpened stone split the tusk in two, scraped out the inside, joined the halves back together and bored a row of carefully placed finger holes. That could easily have taken dozens or even hundreds of hours. One tiny slip, and all that work would crack.

And for what? A flute won't bring down a cave bear. It won't keep the rain off. Its only purpose was to make a sound that felt good to hear in the dark. Consider how wild that is. Back then, a small cut could turn deadly. And still, someone spent all that time and effort on something that existed purely for the mood.

Music did more than fill the silence, though. It held groups together. Play a beat around the fire with other people, and your breathing starts to match theirs, your bodies move together, even your brain activity begins to line up. A scattered band of nervous, vulnerable apes becomes a team. On the longest winter nights, that shared rhythm may have been what kept people from turning on each other.

Still, even the best song gets repetitive. And we're a species built for thrills. Our bodies are designed to handle the rush of chasing down dangerous animals. So picture it: you're warm, you're fed, you're safe, and you haven't felt that rush in days. You're restless. You're itching for something to happen. What fills the gap? A game.

Archaeologists keep turning up one particular kind of object at sites all over the world, separated by vast distances and thousands of years: small ankle bones from sheep, goats and deer. These are astragali, better known as knucklebones. Their uneven shape means they settle on a different side depending on how they're tossed. In other words, they're random. They're dice, made by nature.

And as soon as people noticed that, the betting began. Roll higher than me and you win my arrows. Or my fur. Or my piece of tonight's dinner. Now imagine losing your warm fur on a bad throw, with winter on the way. Thousands of years before coins, casinos or stock exchanges, people were staking the very things that kept them alive on the tumble of a little bone. Greed wasn't really the point. The thrill was. Risk made a quiet evening feel exciting.

Games kept evolving from there. Roughly 5,000 years ago in Egypt, one of the most popular pastimes was Senet, played on a board of 30 squares, with throwing sticks deciding how far your pieces moved. At first it was just a race to the finish. But over many centuries, the game took on a deeper meaning. The board came to represent the underworld, and certain squares became dangers the soul had to get past. Moving your pieces became a kind of practice run for the journey after death. It mattered so much that Senet boards were buried with the dead, including several in the tomb of Tutankhamun. A simple pastime had become something sacred.

Not everyone wants to play, of course. Sometimes you just want to sit back and watch something. And while there were no movie theaters in the Stone Age, there was something not far off.

Deep inside Chauvet Cave in southern France, far from any daylight, the walls are covered with lions, rhinos and horses, painted more than 30,000 years ago. Under modern electric lights, they look like a still gallery. But they were never meant to be seen that way. Their creators saw them by flickering firelight. Move a flame across them, and the animals seem to come alive. Extra legs drawn on top of each other suddenly look like galloping. Shadows shift and stretch. Imagine sitting there in the dark, listening to a story about a hunt while the walls appear to move around you. That's about as close to the first cinema as it gets.

For most of human history, then, entertainment cost nothing. Music, games and stories were homemade and shared. That changed with one invention: the city.

Roughly 6,000 years ago in Mesopotamia, people began leaving small groups of a few dozen and settling into towns of thousands, in places like Uruk. In a band of 40, everyone is family or close to it. You'd never ask a relative to pay you for a song. But a city is full of strangers, and dealing with strangers means trading.

Fairly quickly, fun turned into business. Music now came from paid performers at the tavern. Beer had a price, settled in barley or silver. So did sex. Mesopotamian records mention taverns, drinking debts and paid sex work alongside everyday entries like grain prices and workers' wages. Our oldest cravings had become things you could buy and sell.

What once got people through long Ice Age nights grew into entire industries. Bone flutes led to music streaming. Knucklebones led to Las Vegas. Firelit cave walls led to Netflix picking your next show. The fire is gone. The craving never left.

Because the brain doing the craving is the same one our ancestors had. Inside your head is essentially the same machine that drove someone to carve a flute from a mammoth tusk. So when you find yourself scrolling late at night, chasing one more laugh or one more hit of dopamine, go easy on yourself. Nothing is wrong with you. You're simply doing what people have always done when the sun goes down. You're fighting off the dark.
