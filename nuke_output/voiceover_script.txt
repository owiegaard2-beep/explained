Blowing up cities in videos can be strangely entertaining. There's a raw thrill in watching things explode, and a grim fascination with fireballs, shockwaves and radiation.

And while those videos help put our destructive power in perspective, they're not the best way to grasp what a nuclear explosion really does.

This isn't about comparing stacks of TNT, or measuring how bright a blast is. Nuclear weapons are about people. They're about you.

So let's look at what would actually happen if a single nuclear weapon went off in a major city today. Not a nuclear war. Just one explosion.

Our story begins in the middle of downtown, in a big, busy city. People are heading to work, cramming for exams, lost in their own thoughts and daily routines. Right here, a one-megaton nuclear weapon detonates, and time freezes.

The first phase of the explosion takes less than a second. Within a millisecond, a ball of plasma hotter than the Sun appears and swells into a fireball more than two kilometres wide.

Inside this ball, everyone is simply gone. Picture a drop of water landing on a scorching hot pan. A quick sizzle, and then nothing is left. Most buildings, cars, trees, ugly public sculptures and people... all vaporised.

First comes the flash: a tidal wave of light sweeps across the city in an instant. If your face happens to be turned toward the explosion, you're blinded for several hours.

The heat in that light creates a thermal pulse so intense that it sets things on fire as far as 13 kilometres from ground zero. That means everything that can burn, inside an area of about 500 square kilometres, catches fire. Plastic, wood, clothing, hair and skin.

If you're within reach of the thermal pulse, one moment you're walking to work, and the next, you're on fire.

Now the second phase begins. It unfolds over a few seconds. This is when most people first realise something is wrong, but for hundreds of thousands, it's already too late. After the flash comes the shockwave.

The heat and radiation of the fireball have created a bubble of superheated, highly compressed air around it, and that bubble is now bursting outward. It moves faster than the speed of sound, with winds stronger than any hurricane or tornado. Nothing humans have built can stand up to it.

Within about a kilometre of the fireball, most large buildings are ground down to their foundations. Only steel-reinforced concrete can partly withstand the pressure.

In the nearby parks where retirees were feeding the ducks, trees that were scorched black a second earlier snap like toothpicks. If you're outdoors, you're thrown aside like a speck of dust in a tornado.

The shockwave weakens as it spreads, but around 175 square kilometres of homes still fold like houses of cards, trapping tens of thousands of people who had no time to react. Gas stations explode, and fires spread through the rubble.

Over the next few minutes, a mushroom cloud made of the fireball's remains, dust and ash climbs kilometres into the sky and throws a dark shadow over the ruined city. As it rises, it violently sucks in fresh air from around the city, knocking down even more buildings and feeding the fires with oxygen.

What happens next depends on the city. If there's enough fuel, the fires can merge into a firestorm that consumes the rubble, everyone trapped beneath it, and the people trying to escape.

Up to 21 kilometres away, people just like you rush to their windows to film the mushroom cloud, not realising the shockwave is still on its way. Seconds later, their windows burst into a blizzard of razor-sharp glass.

The third phase plays out over the following hours and days. We're used to thinking that help always arrives, whatever the disaster. This time it's different. A nuclear explosion is like every natural disaster happening at once.

Hundreds of thousands, maybe millions, of people are badly hurt: deep cuts, broken bones, severe burns. Over the next minutes and hours, thousands more die from those injuries.

Countless people are trapped under collapsed buildings, like after an earthquake, or blinded by the flash, deafened by the blast and unable to escape through streets blocked by rubble and debris. They're terrified and confused, with no idea what has happened to them or why.

Many hospitals have most likely been flattened along with everything else, and most doctors and nurses are dead or injured, like everyone around them.

The survivors who were lucky enough to be in subway tunnels, or in just the right spot to escape burns and injuries, aren't out of danger yet.

Depending on the type of weapon, where it detonates and even the weather, a terrible black rain may start to fall, with radioactive ash and dust settling over the city, coating everything and everyone.

Now the silent, invisible and cruel threat of radiation takes over. Every breath carries poison into the lungs of the survivors. Over the following days, those who received the highest doses of radiation will die. And there will be no help, not for hours, maybe not for days.

Society can't function when its infrastructure completely breaks down. Roads are blocked, rail tracks are twisted, and runways are covered in rubble. There's no water, no electricity, no phone network, and no shops to restock supplies.

Rescue teams from nearby cities will struggle to reach the disaster zone, and even when they can, radioactive contamination makes it dangerous to get close. After a nuclear attack, you're on your own.

So, little by little, people climb out of the rubble on foot, covered in radioactive fallout, carrying whatever little they have left. They're slow, injured and traumatised, and every one of them urgently needs food, water and medical care.

And the harm done by a nuclear weapon doesn't stop once the fires die down and the smoke clears. Hospitals in neighbouring cities aren't equipped for a disaster on this scale, and they're overwhelmed by tens or hundreds of thousands of patients with severe injuries.

In the weeks, months and years that follow, many of the survivors will die from cancers such as leukaemia.

Governments don't want you thinking about any of this, because there is no meaningful humanitarian response to a nuclear explosion. There's no real way to help the immediate victims of a nuclear attack.

This isn't a hurricane, a wildfire, an earthquake or a nuclear accident. It's all of those at the same time, only worse. No country on Earth is ready to handle it.

The world has changed in recent years, with leaders once again openly and publicly threatening each other with nuclear weapons. Many experts believe the risk of a nuclear strike is higher now than it has been in decades.

Governments tell their citizens it's good that we have nuclear weapons, but dangerous when anyone else gets them. That threatening others with mass destruction is somehow necessary to keep us safe. But does that make you feel safe?

All it takes is a small group of powerful people going rogue, one small mistake or a simple misunderstanding to unleash a catastrophe on an unimaginable scale.

Blowing things up in videos is fun. Blowing things up in real life, not so much. But there is a solution.

Getting rid of every nuclear weapon, and promising never to build them again.

In 2017, almost two thirds of the world's countries, backed by hundreds of civil society groups and the International Red Cross and Red Crescent Movement, voted for a United Nations treaty to ban nuclear weapons.

This isn't about who has nuclear weapons and who doesn't. The weapons themselves are the problem. They are deeply immoral, and a threat to the survival of all of us.
