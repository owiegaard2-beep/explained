## Part 1 — The law you must apply

**War Powers Resolution of 1973 (50 U.S.C. §§ 1541–1548):**
1. **48-hour rule:** The President must report to Congress within 48 hours of introducing U.S. armed forces into "hostilities" or situations where hostilities are imminent.
2. **60-day clock:** Within 60 calendar days after that report is submitted (or required to be submitted), the President must end the use of forces **unless** Congress has (a) declared war, (b) passed a specific authorization (AUMF), or (c) extended the period by law.
3. **30-day extension:** The President may extend by up to 30 days **only** if he certifies in writing that unavoidable military necessity requires it for the **safe withdrawal** of forces. It is not a free extension for continued operations.
4. **What the law does NOT define:** It never defines "hostilities," and says nothing about whether a **ceasefire pauses** the clock, or whether a President can declare hostilities "terminated" and later start a **new** clock for the same adversary. This ambiguity is the core of the dispute.

**Constitutional background:** Article I gives Congress the power to declare war; Article II makes the President commander-in-chief. Every President since Nixon has claimed the 60-day limit unconstitutionally restricts the commander-in-chief. **No court has ever enforced the 60-day clock.**

**Historical precedent you should weigh:**
- Clinton, Kosovo (1999): air campaign ran ~78 days without authorization.
- Obama, Libya (2011): ran past 60 days; administration argued its air operations did not amount to "hostilities" because U.S. troops faced little risk.
- These show narrow readings of "hostilities" are not new — but you should assess whether 2026 goes further.

## Part 3 — The two competing interpretations

**A. Administration's position ("multiple stretches, each lawful"):**
- A ceasefire pauses the clock (Hegseth).
- Hostilities "terminated" May 1 (Trump letter), so no withdrawal was required.
- Minor exchanges at sea during a truce ("love tap") are not renewed "hostilities."
- A blockade is not "hostilities."
- July 7 was a **new** conflict triggered by Iranian attacks on shipping → new notice → new 60 days.
- Underlying claim: the War Powers Resolution itself is an unconstitutional limit on the commander-in-chief.

**B. Critics' position ("one continuous war, clock never stopped"):**
- Nothing in the 1973 law lets a ceasefire toll the clock (Just Security: the war was "triply illegal" at day 60; JURIST: "the 60-day clock is still running").
- U.S. forces remained deployed, enforced a blockade, struck Iranian territory within weeks of "termination," and threatened resumption — hostilities never ended in substance.
- Restarting the clock by letter would let any President wage indefinite war in 59-day slices, nullifying the law.
- Critics include Democrats, Sen. Susan Collins (R), Rep. Marjorie Taylor Greene (R) (who said the May letter was an effort to restart the clock and dodge a vote), and Rep. Massie (R).
